01 / The Idea

From one idea to three ways of serving.

In 2015, I conceived and co-founded Hola! Churros, combining brand and creative direction with the everyday work of running the business.

Design reached beyond the identity: how customers chose their churro, how an order moved through the kitchen, and how they found us at a busy event.

As Co-founder and Operations Manager, I helped grow the business from a single food truck at events across Santa Catarina into three service formats: the truck, a kiosk at Nações Shopping, and a cart for private and corporate events. I left in late 2017 when I moved to Germany. Hola! Churros kept operating after my departure.

02 / Building from Scratch

Designing a kitchen inside six square meters.

Before opening, we visited food truck events as customers and saw people queue for products that had already sold out. The layout connected space, workflow, and staffing. Explore how a single order moved through the kitchen, and how the team grew with demand.

01 / 04

A Big Idea in a Small Space

We had just six square meters to build a fully functioning churros kitchen. The real challenge wasn’t fitting everything inside. It was making the operation work efficiently.

    Read the kitchen story as text

    The kitchen was a 3 × 2 m food truck, just 6 m², with no gas equipment, laid out and equipped around local food safety and health inspection requirements. I designed its interior around a single principle: each churro moves in one direction. The order is taken at the register. The ticket goes onto a rail above the line, with the oldest orders closest to the toppings station. Then the churro moves through the dough dispenser and fryer, gets rolled in sugar and cinnamon, picks up its filling and toppings, and comes back to the register window for handover.

    Scalability was part of the plan from the start. Minimum and maximum team sizes affected operating and production costs, so the kitchen had to run with a lean team and still make room for more people when demand called for it. On a quiet day, three people ran it: one at the register, one shaping and frying, and one on fillings, toppings, and handover. At a normal event, five people each held their own station. At our busiest events, eight people worked inside at once, with extra hands at dough and frying and at toppings, and one person keeping ingredients and supplies stocked. Customers ordered and picked up at the same window, but different people took the order and handed it over.

    The animation is a 3D reconstruction of the kitchen plan, built for this case study. The people are stock 3D characters (Microsoft Rocketbox), not the real team.

    Designing without a playbook

    When we started building Hola! Churros in 2015, Criciúma had no specific regulatory framework for food trucks.

    We researched regulations from other cities where food trucks were already operating and worked directly with local health authorities to understand how existing food safety requirements could be adapted to a mobile kitchen.

    This collaboration helped us navigate an unfamiliar regulatory environment while making practical decisions about the truck's layout, equipment, and daily operations.

    We weren't just designing a kitchen. We were helping define how this new kind of business could operate.

    03 / Experience Design

    From Observation to Experience

    Running the food truck gave us a firsthand understanding of how customers ordered, how the team worked, and where the experience could be improved.

    Four decisions connected the customer’s choices to the team’s work. Try the complete ordering experience below.

    2. Before the line

      Magnetic menu board

      Products, toppings, and prices were magnets, so the board showed what was actually available that day. Customers could decide before joining the line, not after.

      When an item ran out, its magnet came off the board. No “sold out” signs.

    3. 123

      At the register

      Order tickets

      Pre-printed tickets listed every filling and topping we worked with as a checkbox, plus a blank field for the rotating flavor or an event special.

      Each ticket could hold two churros, since most orders came from couples or pairs.

      1. One filling per churro, marked in its box
      2. Up to two toppings per churro
      3. A numbered stub that tore off for the customer

    4. Behind the counter

      A consistent core

      The number and size of the filling dispensers changed with demand, and we kept spares to add or swap. Nutella and dulce de leche, our best sellers, kept their own dispensers. The others changed with what was available that day.

      Customers built their own churro with one filling and up to two toppings, chosen from as many as twelve.

    5. ORDER

      After paying

      Pickup display

      The person at handover controlled it, so customers could follow their order instead of crowding the counter.

    05 / Expanding into the Mall

    From events to a permanent counter.

    When a major mall, Nações Shopping, was under construction in the city, I researched the commercial conditions on my own initiative and brought them to my partners. The mall offered us attractive conditions for a Hola! kiosk, alongside a franchise investment we decided on together.

    I prepared the commercial proposal and adapted our event cart to the mall, combining its production setup with a counter I designed around the brand’s illuminated chocolate drip.

    Adapting the Concept

    A place in the food court.

    The mall’s initial commercial study proposed a 7 m² cart position measuring 4.15 × 1.70 m. The kiosk was ultimately installed at the location highlighted here.

    The next step was to adapt the mobile setup to a permanent counter while keeping Hola! recognizable.

    Designing the Kiosk

    Originally designed for corporate events, the mobile cart became the foundation for our permanent mall kiosk. Its compact production setup and recognizable visual identity allowed us to adapt the concept to a new retail environment.

    Bringing It to Life

    The cart became part of a larger kiosk: a permanent counter around a mobile production unit, carrying the same identity into the food court.

    View original menu ↗

    A new space.A broader offering.

    The move into the mall also expanded our product range. Alongside our signature filled churros, we introduced Spanish-style churros, Choco Churros and Churros Bites, adapting the menu and visual communication to a broader offering.

    Afterward, we built a second, identical cart to serve the demand for private and corporate events.

    07 / Lessons & Takeaways

    What building a business taught me about design.

    1. 01

      Design starts with understanding people.

      The board, the tickets, and the pickup display came from watching how customers ordered and how the team worked.

    2. 02

      A strong identity needs a system behind it.

      The logo, the drip, and the wave held together across a truck, a kiosk, packaging, and uniforms because they were built as one system.

    3. 03

      Leadership turns ideas into shared execution.

      I learned to give people ownership, keep essential standards clear, and adapt how I supported different team members.

    From buildinga business toleading design.

    I still bring that experience to my work as a designer and team lead: looking at how individual decisions fit together, and giving people room to contribute.
